<h2 id="choose-a-role">Choose by the responsibility you want</h2>

For a frontline character, compare Templar with Gladiator. Templar puts the party's safety and enemy positioning at the center of your decisions. Gladiator suits players who want a more offensive melee identity with defensive options. A tank still needs to learn mechanics: durability does not make a dangerous attack safe for everyone behind you.

For ranged damage, compare Ranger, Sorcerer, and Spiritmaster by how you want to deliver that damage. Ranger favors physical ranged attacks; Sorcerer revolves around spell timing; Spiritmaster adds elemental spirit management. Watching a long boss encounter is more useful than choosing from one spectacular burst clip.

Assassin suits players who enjoy close-range execution, positioning, and committing during attack windows. Practice its movement and target access before investing heavily; unfamiliar boss mechanics and connection delay can change the timing of a rehearsed sequence.

<h3>A two-class tryout with a useful result</h3>

Start with a question you can observe. For Templar versus Gladiator, ask whether you enjoy taking responsibility for where the enemy stands or prefer spending more attention on your own attack opportunities. For Ranger versus Sorcerer, watch whether maintaining distance or finding a safe cast window feels more natural. For Spiritmaster, include managing spirits in the test; a single large damage number does not show that extra work.

Use the same kind of enemy and comparable progression. Note which attacks you can keep using while moving, which mechanic interrupts you, and whether defensive or recovery controls remain easy to reach.

<h2 id="cleric-or-chanter">Cleric or Chanter?</h2>

Choose Cleric if you want the most direct responsibility for healing, cleansing, and recovery. Choose Chanter if you prefer contributing melee damage while improving the party through buffs and situational support. Their responsibilities overlap, but a Chanter build intended for damage should not automatically be treated as a replacement for a dedicated healer.

Some Cleric and Chanter buffs overlap. Before a dungeon, agree who handles recovery and which effects each player supplies. Prepare the support your group needs using the [Chanter guide](/chanter) or [Cleric build guide](/cleric-build).

<h2 id="solo-party-and-pvp">Your main activity changes the decision</h2>

Solo play rewards a character you can operate consistently while handling mechanics yourself. Party play also rewards protection, debuffs, recovery, and reliable positioning. PvP adds target selection and reactions against opponents who do not follow a fixed boss script.

Write down your first priority: relaxed questing, organized dungeons, competitive PvP, or a mixture. Shortlist two classes for that activity. Use the [tier comparison](/tier-list) alongside their roles, then check [builds](/builds) for the skills and options you need.

<h3>Before your first party: assign the work</h3>

A Templar candidate should agree who starts the pull and where the group wants the enemy positioned. A Cleric candidate should make party targeting, a heal, Radiant Recovery for cleansing, and resurrection easy to reach. A Chanter candidate should confirm whether the party expects emergency support or its main recovery. Damage dealers should practice returning to a safe attack position after a mechanic rather than continuing a rehearsed sequence into danger.

**Do I need to pick the highest-ranked class to play with friends?** First check the responsibility the group lacks and whether you enjoy doing it. A needed role you can operate consistently is a more useful starting decision than a letter from a mixed-content ranking.

**What if I like a class but dislike its party job?** Inspect a longer encounter in your intended activity before investing further. Solo comfort alone will not tell you whether you enjoy tank positioning, recovery decisions, or buff management.
